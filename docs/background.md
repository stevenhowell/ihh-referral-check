# Background

Five minutes of context for people who have never worked in homelessness services or healthcare.
Every figure links to its source. Checked October 13, 2026.

## Illumination Health + Home

Illumination Health + Home (IHH), known until 2025 as Illumination Foundation, is a nonprofit based
in Santa Ana. Its stated mission is "to disrupt the cycle of homelessness by providing healthcare,
housing, and hope—together" ([IHH](https://illuminationhealthhome.org/mission-and-vision/)).

It runs recuperative care centers, navigation centers, family emergency homes and permanent
supportive housing, with behavioral health and substance use counseling
([IHH services](https://illuminationhealthhome.org/services/)). Its 2024 Impact Report counts 7,282 clients
served, 2,287 of them in recuperative care ([IHH](https://illuminationhealthhome.org/)).

Two short films about IHH:
[From Streets to Stability](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Jyu6g8q7sWY), on recuperative care, from the California Health Care Foundation (5:38), and
[10 Years of Family Emergency Homes](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FQWH8vxE25I) (3:45).

## What recuperative care is

Recuperative care, also called medical respite, is short-term residential care for "people
experiencing homelessness who are too ill or frail to recover from an illness or injury on the
streets or in a shelter" and "who do not require hospital-level care"
([National Health Care for the Homeless Council](https://nhchc.org/medical-respite/)).

It is a bed, meals and monitoring of a person's health: vital signs, assessments, wound care,
keeping track of medications
([California DHCS policy guide](https://www.dhcs.ca.gov/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/DHCS-Community-Supports-Policy-Guide-Volume-2.pdf)).
It is not a hospital and not a nursing home.

This is why IHH's admission criteria ask whether a person can bathe, walk 100 feet, and take their
own medications. Clients manage their own daily care. IHH's criteria say staff cannot administer
medications. Staff monitor, teach and coordinate.

## Why hospitals send referrals

California law requires every hospital to have a discharge plan for patients who are homeless and
to "identify a postdischarge destination," giving priority to "a sheltered destination with
supportive services." The hospital must give the receiving provider "written or electronic
information about the homeless patient's known posthospital health and behavioral health care
needs" ([Health and Safety Code 1262.5](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?sectionNum=1262.5.&lawCode=HSC)).

That information is the referral packet in Challenge 1.

## Who pays, and why notes matter

In California, Medi-Cal health plans can pay for recuperative care as a "Community Support." In
Orange County the plan is CalOptima Health. Its provider guide says a stay is "auto-authorized for
30 days. If needed beyond 30 days, reauthorization can be submitted in 30-day increments," up to
90 consecutive days, and each request comes with the client's care plan
([CalOptima Health Community Supports Provider Program Guide, January 2026](https://cop-p-001.sitecorecontenthub.cloud/api/public/content/c078b90e18984a0ca200e199f01250be?v=8d577c45)).

So every 30 days, what staff wrote down decides whether a client's stay continues. That is the
paperwork behind Challenge 3.

## The size of the need

Orange County counted 6,321 people experiencing homelessness in January 2026. Of those, 3,065 were
unsheltered ([County of Orange](https://www.ocgov.com/press/county-orange-announces-2026-point-time-count-results)).
Among unsheltered adults, about a third reported a physical disability and about a third a serious
mental illness
([2026 Point in Time Count report](https://ceo.oc.gov/sites/ceo/files/2026-06/2026-Point-In-Time-Count-Report.pdf)).

## Does it work

- A study in Chicago followed 225 hospital patients who were homeless. Those discharged to respite
  care spent fewer days in the hospital over the next year than those discharged to usual care:
  3.7 days against 8.3 ([Buchanan et al., 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16735635/)).
- A review of 13 studies found that medical respite programs "reduced future hospital admissions,
  inpatient days, and hospital readmissions." Results for emergency visits and cost were "mixed
  but promising" ([Doran et al., 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23728025/)).
- A preliminary analysis by California's Medicaid agency found recuperative care "associated with
  a 29.2% net cost reduction"
  ([DHCS fact sheet, October 2025 update](https://www.dhcs.ca.gov/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/Cost-Effectiveness-of-California-Medi-Cal-Community-Supports-Fact-Sheet.pdf)).

## Where the friction is

Researchers who interviewed recuperative care providers and hospitals in California in 2023 heard
the same thing from both sides. Staff "described substantial burdens to receiving/processing
referrals, authorizations, claims, and reimbursements." Hospital staff "felt some recuperative care
programs have onerous eligibility restrictions"
([National Institute for Medical Respite Care, 2024](https://nhchc.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/01/Lessons-Learned-Cal-AIM-implementation-of-recuperative-care-benefit-8.pdf)).
